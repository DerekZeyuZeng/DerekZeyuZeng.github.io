Here is [Andy's page](https://hassiummm.github.io/hassium/math/reading-group/26-27-geometric-invariant-theory/) on this reading group.

Geometric invariant theory begins with a basic question: how can we describe geometric objects up to symmetry by an algebraic variety? Many moduli problems start with a parameter space $X$ and an algebraic group $G$ acting by changes of coordinates. The orbits represent equivalence classes of objects, but the orbit space $X/G$ need not have a satisfactory structure as an algebraic variety. One source of difficulty is that orbits need not be closed: an orbit closure can contain other orbits, reflecting how one object can degenerate into another.

The algebraic starting point is to study functions that are constant along the orbits. For an affine variety $X$ over an algebraically closed field $k$ and a reductive algebraic group $G$ acting on $X$, the ring of invariant functions $k[X]^G$ is finitely generated and defines the affine GIT quotient
$$
X /\!/ G = \operatorname{Spec}\bigl(k[X]^G\bigr).
$$
Each closed point of this quotient corresponds to a unique closed $G$-orbit in $X$. Thus, understanding the quotient requires understanding both invariant functions and the geometry of orbit closures. In the projective setting, a choice of an ample line bundle with a $G$-linearization determines the semistable locus on which the GIT quotient is constructed; the stable locus admits a geometric quotient.

My interest in GIT is closely tied to the question of what a moduli space should remember about the objects it classifies. A GIT quotient can identify distinct orbits and does not retain automorphism groups as part of its moduli data. The quotient stack $[X^{ss}/G]$ keeps track of families of semistable objects and their isomorphisms, making these symmetries part of the geometry.

This leads to the homotopy theoretic aspects of moduli. Over $\mathbb{C}$, the corresponding topological viewpoint is to study homotopy quotients and equivariant cohomology. Even the action of $\mathbb{G}_m$ on a point illustrates the difference: its GIT quotient is a point, while the topological homotopy quotient is $B\mathbb{C}^{\times}\simeq\mathbb{CP}^{\infty}$. Remembering the symmetries can therefore change the topology substantially.

Derived geometry brings in the deformation theory of these objects. Derived intersections record higher intersection data when defining equations fail to meet transversely. For a moduli problem with a derived enhancement, the tangent complex organizes infinitesimal automorphisms, first-order deformations, and obstructions; the full derived structure retains further homotopy-coherent information. I would like to understand how the classical constructions of GIT relate to these richer moduli problems, and how explicit examples reveal which information each construction retains.

Our goal is to understand how these algebraic constructions describe the geometry of moduli problems. We will begin with linear algebraic groups and invariant theory, then move toward affine and projective quotients and the role of stability.

**Prerequisites:** Familier with basic theory of algebraic geometry and homological algebra. Know about Lie groups, Lie algebras and representation theory will be helpful but not required.
