The map is ready. Choose points or turn the derivative angle.

One point leaves one real degree of freedom. The angle φ is arg F′(a). Turning it changes the map while keeping F(a) = b. Requiring F′(a) to be positive real fixes this remaining freedom and determines a unique map.

The formula and why it works

For a = b = 0 and φ = 0, the familiar map is F₀(z) = (2/π) Log((1 + z)/(1 − z)). For arbitrary prescribed points, put η = π Im(b)/2 and use this chain:

ψa(z) = (z − a)/(1 − āz),   q = ei(η + φ)ψa(z),
C = (1 + q)/(1 − q),   H = cos(η) C + i sin(η),
F(z) = Re(b) + (2/π) Log H.

The disk automorphism ψa sends a to 0. Rotation keeps the disk fixed, the Cayley transform sends it onto the right half-plane, and cos(η) > 0 makes H another automorphism of that half-plane. The principal Log has −π/2 < Im Log H < π/2 there. Multiplication by 2/π and real translation therefore give exactly |Im F| < 1.

At z = a, H = eiη, so F(a) = b and F′(a) = [4 cos(η)/(π(1 − |a|²))] eiφ. Every step is biholomorphic; this is a bijection with a holomorphic inverse, not just a deformation of a drawing.

The two marked boundary points lead to Re F → −∞ and +∞. The rest of the boundary approaches Im F = ±1. None of those boundary points belongs to the domain. Colored curves are sampled images under the exact formula; all coordinates use an equal horizontal and vertical scale within each panel. A small outer portion of the disk grid is omitted for legibility, and the strip view is clipped to Re(b) ± 4.

To invert the map, put E = exp(π(w − Re(b))/2), C = (E − i sin η)/cos η, q = (C − 1)/(C + 1), and ζ = e−i(η + φ)q. Then z = (a + ζ)/(1 + āζ).
